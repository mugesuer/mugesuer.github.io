About

Paths & Passions

Originally from Istanbul, I studied Manufacturing Systems Engineering at Sabancı University and worked in finance and project management, including at Pfizer and GlaxoSmithKline. In 2016 I moved to Berlin for a Master's degree in Economics at Humboldt University, and went on to complete my Ph.D. there at the Berlin School of Economics (BSoE), where my research focus sharpened around behavioral questions of gender and identity.

I am now a postdoctoral researcher at the Halle Institute for Economic Research (IWH). My current work combines experimental design and large survey data with LLM-based textual analysis, from how investors judge early-stage ventures to how gender identity shapes labor market outcomes.

Beyond the lab, I'm drawn to Jung, documentary filmmaking, and jogging with my dog Yuki. A curious and proud nerd, I'm fascinated by the psychological underpinnings of social structures.

Job Market Paper · 2026
What Wins Interest Is Not What Closes the Deal: LLM-Measured Judgment in Early-Stage Finance
with Afroza Alam

What makes investors interested in an early-stage venture may not be what makes the deal close. We build a new dataset of 642 pitches from the US television show Shark Tank, following the same ventures from investor interest, through an on-air handshake, to whether that agreement survives due diligence after the show, an outcome no database records. For each pitch, we distinguish realized financial figures (hard information) from three forms of soft information: founders’ forward-looking claims, conventional textual analysis, and contextual judgment. To measure the last, we develop an LLM-based approach that reads each pitch in context and forms a judgment of the venture. Judgment is the strongest predictor of competing investor offers, is associated with better terms for founders, and predicts which ventures survive after the show. It still leads when the handshake is struck, where the stated figures begin to count beside it. Yet it does not predict whether an agreed deal closes. At that stage, founders’ forward-looking claims become predictive instead: larger asks, projections and implied valuations are associated with deals falling through. Different information is predictive at different stages of the same investment process.

Temporal Stability of the Bem Sex Role Inventory (BSRI)
with Stefano Piasenti
Available at SSRN
Abstract

The Bem Sex Role Inventory (BSRI) is among the most widely used measures of gender identity in economic research, yet its original classification has not been tested in over two decades. We provide a contemporary validation of the BSRI’s item classifications using Bem’s original between-subjects elicitation procedure on an online US adult sample. Of the 40 gendered items, 38 retain their original classification: all 20 masculine items are confirmed, and 18 of 20 feminine items remain feminine. No item reverses direction. Yet the magnitude of desirability gaps has gradually attenuated since the original study, with female raters driving much of this compression. Our findings indicate that while the directions of the BSRI remain intact, the intensity of gender differentiation has weakened over time.

2025
Confidence and Information in Strategy-Proof School Choice
with Vincent Meisner, Michel Tolksdorf, and Sokol Tominaj
Available at SSRN
Abstract

Contrary to classical theory, we provide experimental evidence that preference reports in a strategy-proof school-choice mechanism systematically depend on beliefs. We employ a “hard-easy gap” to exogenously vary students’ beliefs about their priority rank. As predicted, underconfidence induces more manipulation and thus more justified envy than overconfidence. The effect of priority information on justified envy crucially depends on the initial beliefs and the real priority ranks: while top students always gain, non-top students lose from this information. In total, correcting overconfidence/underconfidence increases/decreases justified envy. Finally, we confirm that additionally providing information on school availability through a dynamic implementation of the mechanism reduces justified envy compared to priority information alone.

Economic Behavior and Gender Typicality: The Predictive Power of Femininity and Masculinity
with Stefano Piasenti
Available at SSRN
Abstract

Are gender gaps in economic behavior and labor market outcomes associated with biological sex, or with gender-typical traits? Using an online U.S. sample and machine learning, we develop and validate a gender typicality measure capturing masculinity and femininity as separate dimensions. We show that confidence, competitiveness, and risk-taking are associated with masculinity, whereas altruism and equality and efficiency concerns are associated with femininity. Gender typicality also predicts labor market outcomes: masculinity is associated with higher income, managerial positions, performance-based pay, and willingness to negotiate. By leveraging latent heterogeneity, our framework offers a new empirical lens for analyzing gender differences.

2023
Are Women in Science Less Ambitious than Men? Experimental Evidence on the Role of Gender and STEM in Promotion Applications
Single-authored
Rationality & Competition Discussion Paper · Revising manuscript
Abstract

The gender wage gap is to a significant extent driven by gender-based job segregation. One of the potential culprits can be found in supply-side behavioral differences in promotion applications. In this study, using a controlled lab experiment, we disentangle the roles of gender, field of study, and task difficulty in promotion application decisions. Our study provides three crucial findings. First, gender differences in self-limiting promotion application behavior are only present in STEM field students when exposed to a male task. Specifically, when an easier alternative is available, women are less willing to apply for promotions concerning harder tasks than men. Second, there exists no significant difference between men’s and women’s willingness to apply for promotion concerning female jobs in STEM or non-STEM fields. Third, we find that previously reported gender differences in confidence are present only between STEM field students. The results also suggest that self-sorting into positions does not cause a decrease in overall welfare, however, it causes fewer promotions for women in STEM. We finally propose an easy-to-implement policy intervention to close the gender gap in STEM students when applying for a promotion.
